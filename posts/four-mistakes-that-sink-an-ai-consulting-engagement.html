Four Mistakes That Sink an AI Consulting Engagement

The recurring ways London AI consulting projects go wrong, and what a useful outcome actually looks like.

1 November 2026

Most AI consulting in London goes wrong for the same handful of reasons, and almost none of them are technical. The models work. The frameworks are mature. What breaks is the thinking around the project: who is making the call, what they are being told, and whether anyone has asked what the answer is for. These four mistakes show up again and again, and each one is expensive in a different way.

Treating the strategy deck as the deliverable

A document full of ambition is easy to produce and hard to build from. When a roadmap arrives with no architecture, no cost model and no honest timeline, the people who have to implement it quietly disagree with half of it and then do something else. The money is spent, the board has seen the slides, and nothing moves.

The tell is a deliverable your own engineers cannot pick up and start on. A useful result reads like it was written by someone who expected to be in the room when the build began, because the sequencing, the dependencies and the cost assumptions all have to survive contact with reality.

Asking a vendor which vendor you need

Build versus buy is the most expensive decision in most AI projects, and it is routinely made by people whose revenue depends on the answer. Ask a platform which platform you need and you already know what you will hear. The risk is not dishonesty so much as incentive: a recommendation that is technically defensible but shaped by what the adviser sells.

A good outcome here is a recommendation per capability, with the reasoning attached and a clear sense of the point at which the answer would flip. That last part matters more than the verdict itself, because volumes, accuracy requirements and data residency constraints all move over time.

Skipping the readiness question

Teams often start with what they want to build rather than whether their data, their team and their product can support it. The demo works, everyone gets excited, and then accuracy turns out to be worse than anyone measured and inference costs do not scale at the volumes that matter. The feature ends up in a branch nobody wants to own.

An honest read on readiness is uncomfortable and worth paying for. It says what has to change before the thing you want is realistic, and it separates the parts of the plan that depend on engineering from the parts that depend on the business.

Confusing validation with a second opinion

When an AI feature is half-built, the instinct is to bring in someone to confirm it was the right idea. That is not the same as a review. What is actually needed is a senior look at the architecture, at whether the evaluation was ever done properly, and at whether finishing is worth the remaining spend. Sometimes it is, and sometimes the honest answer is to stop.

The pattern across all four is the same: the engagement produces something you can act on and defend, or it produces comfort. Only one of those survives a board question, a procurement review or the first week of a real build.
