Three Ways to Put RAG Behind Your Company Knowledge

A plain comparison of a notebook prototype, an off-the-shelf knowledge tool and a purpose-built retrieval system, and who each one actually suits.

31 October 2026

A team has a folder full of contracts, policies and product notes, and a language model that answers questions about none of them. The obvious question follows: what is the shortest sensible route from that pile of documents to an assistant people trust? In practice, three routes get proposed in London teams, and they are not interchangeable.

The Notebook Prototype

This is the route most engineers try first. A script chunks a folder, embeds the text, and wires the result to a chat window. It is cheap, fast and genuinely useful as a learning exercise. Within an afternoon you can see retrieval working on a clean sample.

It suits one situation: you want to understand the shape of the problem before committing budget, and nobody outside the team will rely on the output. The trouble starts when a prototype like this gets promoted to production without anyone noticing that nothing about it was built for real questions. Retrieval quality, how documents are split, and whether anyone measured the results are the parts that decide whether the thing is useful. A weekend build skips all three, and it shows the first time someone asks something awkward.

The Off-the-Shelf Knowledge Tool

The second route is a subscription product that connects to your drive or wiki and promises answers over your content. Setup is quick, the interface is polished, and for a small, tidy body of documentation it can do a reasonable job.

It suits teams with a modest, well-organised knowledge base, no regulated material, and a tolerance for answers you cannot fully inspect. The limits appear as you grow. You inherit the vendor's choices about chunking, search and which model sees your data. Citations may be vague or missing, so a wrong answer is hard to trace. When your content spans several systems with different formats, the tool's assumptions start to cost you. And if an auditor asks where your customer records went, the honest answer is often that you are not certain.

A Purpose-Built Retrieval System

The third route treats retrieval as the product rather than a feature bolted onto a chat box. That means an ingestion pipeline that keeps documents fresh, hybrid search that combines meaning with keywords and filters, and an evaluation set drawn from your own real questions so you know how often the right passage is actually found.

It suits organisations where the answers matter: compliance teams who need to sign off, support functions answering from real content, and anyone handling sensitive data who must explain exactly where it flows. The cost is real engineering time and an honest measurement phase before launch. The payoff is an assistant whose every answer points back to a source someone can check.

Choosing Between Them

None of these is wrong in the abstract. The mistake is picking the cheapest route for a problem that will be judged on correctness, or paying for a heavy build when a help centre would do the job. A useful test is to ask what happens when the system is wrong: if nobody would notice, a prototype is fine; if someone would act on it, the retrieval layer deserves proper attention.
