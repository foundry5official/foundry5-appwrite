What Should You Ask Before Hiring an AI Development Agency?

The questions that separate a real AI build from an expensive demo, and what a credible answer to each one sounds like.

30 October 2026

Every agency in London now claims to build AI. The pitch decks are similar, the language is the same, and the demos usually work. The difference only shows up later, when the feature is live, the bill arrives, and the model says something wrong to a real customer. These are the questions worth asking before you sign, and what a good answer depends on.

What Happens When the Model Gets It Wrong?

This is the question most teams forget to ask, and it is the one that decides whether your product survives contact with users. An LLM that is right most of the time still produces confident errors, and in a support bot or a document pipeline those errors land on your customers.

A good answer depends on whether anyone has thought about accuracy as a measurable thing rather than a vibe. You want to hear how errors are caught before launch, how edge cases are handled, and who is responsible when the model drifts after go-live. If the response is that modern models are very accurate, that is not an answer. It is a hope.

Who Owns the Code When the Contract Ends?

AI builds often wrap a model behind a product surface, and that wrapper is the part you paid for. If you do not own it outright, you are renting your own feature.

The answer here is usually a matter of contract rather than intent, so it is worth being direct about it early. A partner who builds for handover will talk comfortably about your engineers maintaining the system, about documentation, and about what happens if you stop working together. One who deflects is telling you something.

Why Does the Cost Estimate Look Fine Now and Scary Later?

Inference is cheap in testing and expensive at scale, and the gap between the two surprises a lot of teams. What looks like a modest per-request cost becomes a serious line item once usage grows, especially without caching or model routing in place.

A credible answer depends on whether cost has been treated as an engineering problem from the start. Ask what keeps the bill predictable as volume rises, and whether there is a plan for the moment the numbers stop making sense. Optimisation is much easier to design in than to bolt on afterwards.

Is a Custom Build Even the Right Call?

Sometimes the honest answer is no, and an agency willing to say so is worth more than one that agrees with everything. Off-the-shelf tools cover plenty of ground, and a custom model is not automatically better than a well-configured product.

A good answer depends on the problem you actually have. If AI is there for a funding narrative rather than a user need, that is a different project. A partner who asks what the feature is meant to do, and what happens if you do not build it, is doing the work of evaluation rather than sales.

What Does a Finished Build Include?

Launch day is not the end of an AI system. Models drift, data changes, and the prompts that worked in month one quietly stop working in month six.

A solid answer depends on what arrives alongside the code. Monitoring, failover, documented accuracy and latency figures, and a support window after go-live are the difference between a product and an experiment. Ask what you receive at handover and what happens in the first month afterwards. The answer tells you whether the build was designed to be run.
